# Literature Review


## Literature Review

The growing complexity of global transportation networks has increased the need for greater transparency, responsiveness and resilience in fleet management. Traditional fleet management systems, often characterised by fragmented data and delayed information flows, are unable to effectively address current challenges such as operational disruptions, fluctuating demand and sustainability imperatives . Hence, artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a game-changing enabler of real-time fleet management visibility and monitoring.

Past literature highlighted the importance of AI in fleet management for power utilities and emphasised that applying machine-learning and data-analytics techniques to telematics, sensor-derived and vehicle-operational data, utilities can predict maintenance requirements, allocate service vehicles proactively and reduce unplanned downtime .

Early research highlights the importance of AI in predictive analytics and decision support, including route optimisation, maintenance forecasting and risk identification in fleet operations . These studies show how machine learning algorithms may use large-scale historical and real-time data to increase forecast accuracy and operational efficiency. Similarly, IoT-enabled systems have been acknowledged for giving detailed, sensor-based insights into logistics and asset tracking . When integrated with AI, IoT data streams enable proactive disruption response and the automation of complex fleet management decisions .

The necessity for utilities to provide real-time fleet tracking and visibility systems that go beyond location awareness has increased due to recent interruptions, which range from extreme weather to the grid-edge integration of distributed energy. These technologies, which have historically been employed in logistics, are now becoming essential grid-operations tools that allow utilities to find personnel, project restoration schedules, safely negotiate dangers, and interface with work management and outage systems . Despite its rapid advancement, the low-latency predictive and prescriptive environment created by integrating artificial intelligence (AI) with multi-modal data sources, such as GPS, telematics, dashcam or UAV imagery, SCADA and AMI event streams, work-order systems and external layers like weather and wildfire risk, still faces practical challenges like cybersecurity, interoperability and maintaining human-in-the-loop decision integrity .

Architecturally, new utility fleet visibility technologies combine edge and cloud computing. Vehicles and roadside sensors process perceptual data, such as hazard detection and vehicle identification and only the most important compressed aspects are exchanged with cloud systems for overall optimisation and dispatch coordination. This design ensures that safety-critical choices are made in real time despite poor connectivity, while dispatch-level orchestration continues unabated . AI plays three critical roles in fleet operations: perception (e.g., vision models for hazard detection, signal processing for telematics), prediction (e.g., ETA and restoration time estimation) and prescription (e.g., dynamic routing and resource staging), resulting in tight integration with grid outcomes .

With machine learning algorithms that can determine operational status such as whether a bucket truck is laden, idle or going through adverse events based only on high-frequency CAN, GPS and telematics data, telematics analytics have advanced. By detecting fuel or idle waste in almost real time, insights from such systems can directly improve utility fleet safety, productivity and efficiency . In addition to telematics, GPS/AVL and V2X pipelines have been created to facilitate connectivity in coverage-degraded situations, which are common during storms. These frameworks have low-latency streaming back-ends, robust cellular hardware and lightweight protocols to guarantee that positional and vehicle-health updates reach dispatch dashboards in less than a second .

On the perception front, edge analytics techniques communicate compressed characteristics to cloud infrastructure for greater situational awareness and enable instant risk detection like speeding in work zones or breaching protective barriers through onboard inference on video streams. During network outages, this dual-layer processing not only speeds up response times but also uses less bandwidth . Similarly, vision AI research shows strong performance, with YOLOv8 achieving over 90% mean average precision (mAP) in visual detection and over 96% when fusing thermal data. This research is mostly focused on utility infrastructure such as power lines. The perception pipelines of these studies can be modified to ground-level dangers, crew safety and terrain mapping for navigation, even though their primary focus is aerial inspection .

In storm restoration scenarios, when AI-driven routing and dispatch greatly enhance repair procedures, the strategic benefit of real-time fleet visibility is especially evident. Road damage, energised switching, staff availability and material limits are all co-optimized by exact and heuristic optimisation models. While Cavdar create faster dynamic programming methods, Shuai introduce routing logic to reduce outage time. By combining scheduling and routing with grid-level restoration measurements, Sinha go one step further and allow repair solutions that correspond with the highest megawatt restoration per unit time. Additional improvements include transformer-based models that use past outage data and streams of real-time telemetry to predict anticipated times of restoration (ETR), giving dispatchers precise and timely restoration predictions .

These AI solutions need to be integrated with current operations technology stacks, including OMS, DMS, AMI and DERMS, in order to be deployed successfully. Utility programs highlight AI literacy, governance and change management as critical for scaling and they support gradual experiments that emphasise worker augmentation rather than complete replacement . Fleet electrification frameworks that combine routing with charging infrastructure, depot power management and V2X limitations are among EPRI’s parallel initiatives. These frameworks are particularly crucial as utilities transition to electric vehicle fleets .

During storms, situational intelligence is also supported at scale by real-time fleet systems. A closed feedback loop for ongoing capability improvement is formed by pre-storm staging, event-based triage utilising damage and path inference and post-event model retraining. AI-enhanced fleet visibility can lower costs and increase coordination during harsh weather, as demonstrated by trade publications . Further evidence that on-site processing preserves operational consistency even in the event of centralised network failures comes from edge and Internet of Things traffic monitoring investigations .

In order to properly guide staff, emerging research approaches include risk-aware fleet navigation, which integrates real positional data with hazard layers such as flooding, downed conductors or wildfire danger. According to Santos , these systems have the ability to dynamically modify safety envelopes in response to observed near misses and environmental changes. Additional factors are brought to light by the electrification of utility fleets: route planning now needs to take charging status, depot energy supply and pricing structures into account, which calls for cooperation with distribution-level DERMS . With federated learning, ETA and hazard models can be improved cooperatively without exchanging raw data, providing a potential method for privacy-preserving model training across contractor networks and mutual aid agreements . Furthermore, even in situations where GNSS is unreliable, continuous coverage is ensured using geo-distributed edge observability techniques that coordinate vehicle tracking across yard cameras, substation nodes and mobile relays .

However, there are still a number of implementation issues. Heterogeneous device ecosystems and varying schema across utility and contractor systems continue to pose major challenges to data interoperability. The task of integrating telematics, AVL and OMS datasets is not simple . Secure edge provisioning, model integrity checks and hardened pipelines are required due to cybersecurity issues such as GPS spoofing, video-stream manipulation and command-and-control vulnerabilities . For sustained deployment, human-centric concerns including change management, pilot-first tactics and preserving model trust as storm situations change are essential . Additionally, studies that quantify real decreases in SAIDI/SAIFI measurements, enhancements in crew safety and emissions advantages need longitudinal validation in utility contexts. Utility-tailored, multi-event performance evaluations are required, notwithstanding the promising results of optimisation and routing studies .

All things considered, utility fleet operations are being transformed into specialised grid-resilient systems by the convergence of developments in AI, telematics, IoT and edge computing. In addition to real-time tracking, these technologies allow for predictive coordination based on grid goals. Industry guidelines offer adoption pathways and restoration outcomes are now supported by route planning models, vision-based perception and edge analytics. The convergence of risk awareness, federated intelligence, electrification logistics, grid awareness and rigorous effect evaluation in operational situations should be the main focus of future study.
